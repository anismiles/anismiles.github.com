Baselayer: from bank onboarding to agent identity

How a KYB startup built a business graph, then pointed it at AI agents

Baselayer sells banks a fast answer to one question: is this business real and safe to take on? In three years it went from a founder's own banking problem to a pitch for verifying AI agents. By the end you'll know what it built, how it grew so fast, and why the agent move is smaller and safer than it sounds.

Who this is for: founders and product leaders who want the company's arc in ten minutes. Section 1 and section 2 cover the core business; section 3 and section 4 cover the 2026 agent products and the risks. It's built from press releases and trade coverage, not insider data.

1 · A banking problem

early 2023 to the seed round

In early 2023, Jonathan Awad and Timothy Hyde couldn't open a business bank account after a run of bank failures. Other young companies were stuck the same way, unable to pay bills or vendors. The slow part was KYB, short for Know Your Business: the checks a bank runs to confirm a company exists, who owns it, and whether it's a fraud risk. Banks often took days or weeks.

Awad became CEO and Hyde CTO. Some records list a third co-founder, William Slessman. The founding date is fuzzy: most coverage says 2023, while the company's own 2026 release says 2024.

Baselayer left stealth in May 2024 with a $6.5M seed round and almost 30 customers, including the business bank Rho and a large loan-servicing platform. One angel was Eric Woodward, former president of Early Warning Services, the bank-owned network behind Zelle. That choice fits the pitch. From the start, Awad sold fraud data shared across banks, something no single bank can build alone. The timeline below shows the four stages since.

2 · From checklist to credit file

2024 to 2026

After the seed, the product grew from a yes-or-no identity check into a full business risk file. The product list now covers lien search and filing, lawsuits and bankruptcies, website and review analysis, ongoing monitoring for sanctions and ownership changes, and an AI assistant that does routine underwriting steps. That puts Baselayer closer to a business credit bureau than to a form-checking tool.

The customer count jumped from about 30 to more than 2,300 institutions, which the company describes as one in five in the US. Tracxn put headcount at 28 people in May 2026. A team that size doesn't sign thousands of banks one by one. The likely engine is partners. The head of FIS's ChexSystems unit, which sells business account screening to banks, says on Baselayer's homepage that the joint product serves hundreds of institutions. The lending software firm LoanPro embeds it too. That reading is the author's, not a disclosed figure. The diagram below shows the flow.

This loop is the moat. Anyone can buy public records. Few can get thousands of banks to report the fraud they see into one pool.

3 · The agent bet

september 2026

On 22 September 2026, Baselayer announced a $35M Series A led by M13, with Torch, Picus, Afore and Socure's Matt Thompson. The funding trail is messy. In August, citybiz reported a $20M round led by Koro Capital and M13, and Crunchbase lists an unconfirmed $20M seed from 2024. Neither appears in the company's release, and reported totals range from about $40M to $47M.

The round came with an Agentic Identity Suite. Its centre is KYA, Know Your Agent: proof of which platform runs an agent, which business or person gave it authority, and whether that authority can be shown. The proof is a signed digital credential with an expiry date and a fixed scope, built on a W3C standard. Around it sit a registry of verified businesses, counterparty checks, and an MCP server (a standard way for AI agents to call outside tools) that lets agents pull Baselayer's business data. Named partners include FIS, Prove, Socure, Exa and Nevermined.

M13's Karl Alomar said he first saw a company solving a KYB banking problem, and changed his view once agent payments appeared. So the round was priced on the agent story. The stack below shows why that story is lower risk than it looks.

An agent credential is only as good as the check on the business behind it. Baselayer already runs that check at thousands of institutions, so it enters the agent market with data and distribution rather than a whiteboard.

4 · What to watch

open questions

Who sets the credential format? Forkast counts five agent identity products shipped in five weeks, none of which work with each other. Visa, Mastercard and Ant International have also announced a joint framework. If the card networks own the format, Baselayer becomes one checker among several rather than the issuer.

Which half pays first? Selling verified business data to agents through MCP and Exa is a new sales channel for a product that already works. The credential business depends on standards and adoption that don't exist yet. In the author's view, the data half earns money sooner.

Identity isn't behaviour. Awad has said the product won't stop a model from ignoring instructions or being exploited. KYA tells you who's accountable after the fact. It doesn't make the agent safe.

How firm are the numbers? The 2,300 institutions and the claim of more than $1 billion in prevented fraud losses are the company's own figures. Neither has been independently checked.
