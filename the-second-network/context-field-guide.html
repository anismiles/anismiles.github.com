Context: What Do They Mean When They Say It?

Ten things called context, how they fit together, and which ones carry weight

In the space of a year, "context" became the most used word in enterprise AI. It now names a token buffer, a wire protocol, a file in a code repository, a semantic layer, a memory store, an investment thesis and a startup category. This essay separates them, shows how they connect, and offers a way to tell a working idea from a new label.

A note on bias: the author builds data platform software in this category. This page names none of those products and argues for no vendor, including his own. Where one vendor is quoted criticising another, both sell in the same market, and we say so.

1 · One word, ten jobs

why the word stopped meaning one thing

For most of the history of large language models, "context" had a narrow technical meaning: the text a model could see on a single call. That changed in mid-2025, when practitioners started using context engineering as a name for the harder work of deciding what that text should be. Within months the word had spread outward, from the model to the tools that feed it, to the data platforms behind those tools, and finally to the business pitches built on top of all of them. By mid-2026, O'Reilly's survey of the agent stack described context engineering as having replaced prompt engineering as the core discipline, and every data platform keynote was framing agent quality as a context problem.

The result is at least ten distinct uses of one word. They aren't competing definitions of the same thing. They sit at different distances from the model, they're built at different times, and they serve different people. Most of the confusion comes from someone taking the word from one layer and using it to sell a product that lives in another.

The diagram reads best from right to left. The model only ever sees the window, the text it receives on one call. That window is filled on every call by a process of assembly, which is what context engineering names. Assembly pulls its material through a wire, now usually the Model Context Protocol, from a set of sources that were built before anyone asked a question: procedures, definitions, memory, records of past decisions, and the ordinary documents and conversations of a company. Three consequences follow, and each one clears up a common misunderstanding.

MCP is not a context strategy. MCP is the pipe. It gives an agent a standard way to reach a system, but it carries whatever that system holds. If the warehouse has four columns that could each be called "revenue" and no record of which one finance uses, MCP delivers that ambiguity to the agent faster and more reliably. An organisation that adopts MCP without fixing its sources has built a quicker route to the same wrong answer.

Context engineering can't be bought off the shelf. It's a per-call practice: choosing what goes into the window for this step of this task, in what order, within a limited budget of tokens. Tools can make it easier, and good frameworks help. But the quality of the result depends almost entirely on what the sources contain. An agent assembling context from undefined, unowned sources will assemble undefined, unowned context, however elegant the assembly code.

A product sold as "the context layer" is a claim about several sources at once. When a product presents itself as the context layer, it's claiming to own some combination of the boxes on the left: procedures, semantics, memory, decision traces and knowledge. That claim is worth checking one box at a time. In practice most products are strong in one or two of them and thin in the rest, and knowing which is which tells you what else you'll need.

2 · Two questions that sort every use

when is it built, and who is it for

You can place almost any use of the word by asking two questions. The first is when it's built. Some context exists only for the duration of a call. Some accumulates continuously as people and agents use a system. And some is written deliberately, before any question exists, by someone accountable for it. The second question is who it serves: a single call, a single user or team, or the whole enterprise. Plotting the ten meanings against those two axes produces the map below.

Select a dot to read a short summary of that meaning.

Two patterns stand out. The first is that every packaging claim (the hollow dots) sits in the upper right, in the durable, enterprise-wide corner. That's where budgets and long contracts are, so it's where marketing concentrates. The second is that the middle column, context learned from use, is the youngest and least settled part of the map. Memory and decision traces are where the hardest unsolved problems are, and where the evidence is thinnest.

3 · Ten meanings, from the model outward

what each one is, where it comes from, and what to ask

The entries below follow the order of the first diagram, starting inside the model and moving outward to the business pitches. Each opens with its position on the map, then covers what the term means in practice, where it came from, what it's commonly confused with, and how much weight it can bear. Each ends with one question worth asking when someone uses the term on you.

3.1 The context window

Builtper callServesone callStandardnone; set by each model

The context window is the text a model can attend to on a single call: the system prompt, the conversation so far, any retrieved passages, the results of tool calls, and the model's own earlier output. It's counted in tokens, and it disappears when the call ends. Everything else on this page is, in one way or another, a method for deciding what ends up inside it.

Windows have grown from a few thousand tokens to a million or more, and it's tempting to read that growth as the end of the problem. The research says otherwise. Liu and colleagues found that models use information at the start and end of a long input far more reliably than information in the middle, a pattern that mirrors the serial-position effect in human recall. Chroma's Context Rot study tested 18 models and found that every one of them degraded as input length grew, even on simple retrieval tasks. A larger window is a larger desk. It isn't a better filing system.

The window is most often confused with memory. The difference is time: the window holds things for one call, while memory holds them across calls. A typical failure is a long policy document pasted in whole, with the one clause that matters buried two-thirds of the way through.

SignalThis is the original meaning of the word and the only one that can be measured directly. When a claim about context can be tested against the window, test it there.

AskWhere in the window does this land, and what did you leave out to make it fit?

3.2 Context engineering

Builtper callServesone agent stepStandardnone; a practice

Context engineering is the practice of deciding what goes into the window for each step of a task. Take an agent asked why customer churn rose in March. Before the model reasons at all, something has to decide whether to include the churn metric's definition, last month's analysis, the schemas of three candidate tables, a note about a pricing change, or none of these, and in what order, within a fixed token budget. That decision is context engineering.

The term took hold in June 2025. Shopify's Tobi Lütke said he preferred it to "prompt engineering," and Andrej Karpathy agreed, describing it as "filling the context window with just the right information for the next step." Anthropic's engineering team frames the same idea as spending a finite attention budget, and LangChain's reference material breaks the work into writing, selecting, compressing and isolating context. By 2026 it had its own books, including Boni García's from Manning.

It's often confused with the sources it draws on. Context engineering is how an agent spends context; it doesn't produce the definitions, memory or records it spends. The characteristic failure is excellent assembly over poor material: a carefully tuned retrieval pipeline that faithfully delivers three conflicting definitions of the same metric.

Signalas a practice, and a real engineering skill. Noiseas a product category. A "context engineering platform" is usually retrieval or memory tooling under a new name, because the practice itself lives in each builder's code.

AskIs this something my engineers do, or something you sell? If you sell it, which source in section 1 is it really?

3.3 The Model Context Protocol

Builtper callServesany agentStandardMCP, under the Linux Foundation

The Model Context Protocol is an open protocol that gives agents a common way to reach tools, data and applications. A host application runs a client, which connects to servers that expose tools and resources. Anthropic released it in November 2024 and, in December 2025, donated it to the Agentic AI Foundation, a fund under the Linux Foundation co-founded by Anthropic, Block and OpenAI. By then there were more than 10,000 public MCP servers, and the protocol was supported in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Cursor and VS Code.

MCP solved a real problem. Before it, every agent needed a custom integration for every system. But the word "context" in its name has done a lot of marketing work. MCP carries context; it doesn't create it. A connection to a warehouse tells an agent what tables exist. It doesn't tell the agent which of them finance trusts, or what a column called rev_adj means.

MCP is most often confused with the context it carries. The typical failure is an agent that reaches the right system through a well-built MCP server and still picks the wrong column, quickly and confidently.

Signalas plumbing, and now the default plumbing. "We have an MCP server" is a statement about reachability. It says nothing about whether the answers will mean what your business means.

AskWhat does the agent actually receive through this server: raw rows, or definitions it can rely on?

3.4 Instruction and procedural context

Builtbefore the questionServesa team or repositoryStandardAGENTS.md; Agent Skills

Procedural context tells an agent how work is done. In software, it's a file such as AGENTS.md at the root of a repository, explaining how to build, test and lint the project, or an equivalent like CLAUDE.md or an editor's rules file. Agent Skills go a step further, packaging instructions together with scripts and reference material that an agent loads when a task calls for them. OpenAI's AGENTS.md was contributed to the same foundation as MCP, and the two are complementary: MCP provides connections, while procedural context provides the know-how for using them.

The enterprise version of this layer is larger and mostly unwritten: how a refund above a threshold gets approved, which contract template legal prefers for which counterparty, who to escalate to when a shipment is held. Memory research distinguishes knowing that from knowing how, and most enterprise writing on context concentrates on the first while agents most often fail on the second.

Procedural context is often confused with semantic context. Semantics says what a term means; procedure says what to do with it. The characteristic failure is drift: two skills that give contradictory instructions, or a file that described last quarter's process and was never updated.

Signaland underrated. This is the "how we do things here" layer, and it deserves the same ownership and review that code gets.

AskWho owns these files, who reviews changes to them, and what happens when two of them disagree?

3.5 Semantic context

Builtbefore the questionServesthe whole enterpriseStandardemerging; mostly platform-native

Semantic context is authored meaning: metric definitions, dimensions, entities and the relationships between them, sometimes formalised as an ontology. When an agent is asked for last quarter's revenue, semantic context is what tells it that the company means recognised revenue, on the fiscal calendar, net of refunds, from the finance model rather than the sales one. It's written once, versioned, and served to every dashboard and agent so that the same question gets the same number.

In 2026 every major platform shipped a version of it. Snowflake made its Semantic Views queryable in standard SQL in March, and Databricks made Unity Catalog Business Semantics generally available in April, about a month apart. Databricks also said it would open-source the core into Apache Spark. Microsoft's Fabric IQ adds an ontology of business entities over OneLake. The vendor-neutral Open Semantic Interchange effort aims to let definitions move between these systems, though one vendor's review found that no product yet ships its import or export.

Expectations should stay grounded. Databricks' own agent metadata consists of display names, formats and synonyms: useful, and modest. Semantic context is often confused with a data catalog, but they do different jobs. A catalog describes what assets exist and who owns them. A semantic layer defines how a number is computed. The characteristic failure is fragmentation: two engines, each with its own definition of the same metric, both certified.

SignalThe most mature and most testable layer on this page. Claims of complete accuracy from semantics alone are slogans, but the layer itself is where accuracy is most reliably won.

AskWhere does this definition live when we run more than one engine, and who approves a change to it?

3.6 Agent memory

Builtfrom useServesone user or agentStandardnone

Agent memory is what a system learns from use and keeps across sessions: a user's preferences, facts they've stated, earlier answers, and corrections. If a user tells an assistant their sales region has changed, memory is what should make next week's report reflect it. Consumer versions ship inside ChatGPT and Microsoft Copilot. Developer versions include Mem0, Zep and Letta. By 2026, O'Reilly was describing memory as a first-class architectural primitive rather than an afterthought bolted onto a vector database.

The hard part is the write path. A memory system has to decide what to capture, how to reconcile it with what it already holds, when to let it decay, and what to do when a stored fact becomes wrong. Some systems overwrite; better ones mark the old fact invalid and keep a record of when it was true. Whatever is discarded at write time can't be recovered when a question arrives.

Memory is often confused with semantic context. The difference is authority: semantic context is authored and correct for everyone, while memory is learned and useful for one relationship. The characteristic failure is the stale fact, such as the old sales region, still retrieved confidently weeks after the user corrected it.

Signal, noisy evidenceThe problem is real, and the measurement is immature. Most memory benchmarks reward recall and say little about updates, contradictions or knowing when not to answer. Headline scores often come from press releases using a different model from the paper they cite.

AskWhen a remembered fact becomes wrong, is it overwritten or marked invalid, and can I see what it used to say?

3.7 Decision traces and context graphs

Builtfrom useServesthe whole enterpriseStandardnone

A decision trace records why an action was allowed: which policy version applied, what exception was granted, who approved it, and what precedent was used. Consider a discount approved by a vice president in a video call or a direct message. The CRM shows the final price. It doesn't show who approved the deviation or why. That missing record is what a decision trace captures.

In December 2025, Foundation Capital named the accumulated structure of these traces a "context graph" and argued that systems of record for decisions could become the next generation of large enterprise platforms. The firm's Ashu Garg later sized the opportunity against the trillions enterprises spend on salaries and services rather than the software market. Not everyone accepts the novelty: one Finextra essay points out that capital markets firms have kept decision trails for years inside time-series systems, without calling them graphs.

Decision traces are often confused with knowledge graphs. A knowledge graph models how things relate; a decision trace records why something happened. A useful trace, sometimes called a correction record, needs four parts: who made the change, when, why, and what it replaced. The characteristic failure is a trace without provenance, which quickly becomes indistinguishable from rumour.

Signalfor the idea. Systems of record store what happened, not why, and a trail of reasoning is one of the few assets a competitor can't recompute. Framingfor the label. A decision trace doesn't need a graph to be useful, and the trillion-dollar sizing belongs to an investment thesis rather than an architecture.

AskShow me one trace. Who wrote it, when, why, and what did it replace?

3.8 The context layer Packaging

Builtbefore the questionServesthe whole enterpriseStandardnone

The context layer is a claim that a single product can serve governed meaning to agents at runtime. One typical definition, from Tellius, bundles five things: semantic definitions, entity resolution across systems, governance over what agents may do, lineage for how an answer was reached, and memory of past decisions. That's most of the left-hand side of the first diagram.

The term is used most by catalog, metadata and semantic-layer vendors, and the relabelling has been literal. OpenMetadata 2.0, released in August 2026, now describes itself as an open context layer for data and AI rather than a data catalog. Vendors are candid about each other: Strategy and Zaimler both describe the trend as a homepage rename, while each sells its own context layer. DataHub makes the sharpest point: neither a semantic layer nor a context layer creates the governed metadata it depends on.

The concept is real when three things are true: entities are resolved across systems, access is enforced on every query at runtime, and the result can be served to agents from any vendor. It's most often confused with a catalog. Kaelio draws the line usefully: the catalog is a system of discovery, and the context layer should be a system of governed execution. The characteristic failure is a catalog with an MCP server attached, sold as something more.

Mostly packagingThe need is real; the label is mostly applied to products that haven't changed underneath.

AskTake away the MCP server. What does this product do that last year's version didn't?

3.9 Platform umbrella brands Packaging

Builtbefore the questionServesthe whole enterpriseStandardplatform-specific

The largest platform vendors now bundle several of these layers under one name. Microsoft's version splits it four ways: Work IQ for how employees work, Foundry IQ for an organisation's documents and policies, Fabric IQ for business entities and data, and Web IQ for the web. Foundry IQ in particular turns enterprise content into permission-aware knowledge bases that agents can query. Databricks built its June 2026 summit around the same idea, launching Genie Ontology as a live context layer, while Ali Ghodsi framed AI's difficulty as a context problem rather than an intelligence problem.

These are real products under marketing names, and strategically they're the most important entry on this page. Context is moving inside the platform. For an organisation that runs on one vendor's estate, that's convenient: definitions, permissions and agents share one governance model. For an organisation that runs several engines, or uses agents the platform didn't build, it raises a portability question that the umbrella brand tends to leave unasked.

Platform bundles are often confused with neutral standards. They may use open protocols at the edges, but the definitions inside are shaped for the platform's own agents. The characteristic failure is a metric defined perfectly in one platform and invisible to the agent running in another.

Real products, marketing namesEvaluate each component on its own; the umbrella name adds nothing to the assessment.

AskDoes this context serve agents that aren't yours, with the same definitions and the same permissions?

3.10 The company brain Packaging

Builtfrom use and before the questionServesteams and the enterpriseStandardnone

A company brain is a system that ingests documents, chat, meetings and tickets, keeps memory, and serves the result to people and agents with permissions attached. The term hardened in 2026. Y Combinator's Summer 2026 request for startups included an entry asking for company brain businesses, startups such as Hyper adopted the phrase directly, and a company named simply Brain launched in September.

Underneath the name is a familiar combination: enterprise search, memory and permissions over unstructured content. One vendor writing about the category concedes as much, describing the architecture, stripped of marketing terms, as a learning memory layer at organisational scale.

The metaphor also misleads. The model is already the part that reasons. What an enterprise lacks is governed, addressable memory for that reasoning to draw on, with a clear line between what was written by someone accountable and what was picked up from a chat thread. The characteristic failure is exactly that line going missing: an agent that can't tell official policy from an opinion posted in a channel.

Mostly marketingThe underlying capabilities are useful. The category name describes an aspiration, not an architecture.

AskWhich of these answers were written by someone accountable, which were learned from use, and can I tell them apart?

5 · Five tests

separating a working layer from a new label

The ten meanings above are easier to tell apart in principle than in a product description. The five tests below work on any claim involving context, whatever layer it comes from. They're ordered so that the first and last catch relabelling, while the middle three catch products that are honest but early.

The first test is schema. A working layer can say exactly what it stores and show you its shape: a definition has fields, a memory has a structure, a trace has parts. "Your organisational knowledge" is a description of an ambition, not a schema. The second is provenance. There should be a write path that records who added or changed something, when, and why, because without it nothing can be audited, rolled back or trusted over time.

The third test is policy. Permissions should be enforced at the moment an agent retrieves, on every query, rather than filtered out of a prompt afterwards. An agent that has already seen a restricted number can't be made to forget it. The fourth is evidence. Any claim about accuracy or cost should come with a baseline that someone outside the company making the claim could rerun.

The fifth is the swap test, and it's the quickest. Replace the word "context" in the description with "metadata" or "search." If the description still reads as accurate and nothing about the product seems new, the word was doing the work rather than the product.

6 · What to discount

phrases and numbers that carry little weight

Some uses of the word carry almost no information, and they're worth discounting on sight. Trillion-dollar sizing of a category that doesn't yet have an agreed schema is one. Promises of complete accuracy are another; no layer on the map delivers that, and the ones that come closest make narrower claims. The company brain as a category name, the context layer label on a catalog that hasn't changed underneath, "context engineering platform" on a retrieval product, and new IQ-style names for existing services all belong in the same group. None is necessarily attached to a bad product. They just tell you nothing about it.

Numbers deserve the same care, because a few of them travel from post to post without their origins. The most quoted is a prediction that 60% of agentic analytics projects relying only on MCP will fail by 2028 for lack of a consistent semantic layer. It comes from a Gartner analyst's talk at the firm's 2026 data and analytics summit, and it's usually cited secondhand, through vendor posts. It's a forecast, not a finding. Similarly, two vendors each report a 38% accuracy gain: one from grounding agents in governance metadata, another from unified context across 522 queries. They may describe the same study or two different ones, and neither has been independently reproduced. Vendor self-measurement is a claim until someone else runs it.

7 · What stays open

where the real work is

Strip away the packaging and the unsolved problems sit in the middle and right of the map. The first is the relationship between authored meaning and learned memory. When an official definition says one thing and a hundred user corrections say another, which wins, and how would anyone know? The second follows from it: how a correction from one user can become an official definition without letting anyone rewrite the business by repetition.

The third is the difference between what an agent may see and what it may rely on. Access control answers the first question well. Almost nothing answers the second. And the fourth is portability. As context moves inside each platform, someone still has to serve the agents that span several platforms, or that no platform built.

These are questions of architecture. No amount of renaming answers them.
