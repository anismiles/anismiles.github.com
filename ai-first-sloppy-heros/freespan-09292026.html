Freespan: how it looks from outside, and the team it needs next

A read of Freespan's public website, and a plan for its first engineering and FDE team

Retailers, job candidates and investors will look at Freespan's website before they meet anyone. This page shows what they will see, which claims they will check first, and what team Freespan needs to back those claims up.

A note on bias: the author co-founded a data platform company that works close to the semantic layer market Freespan describes. So the author has a view on this market and may overlap with Freespan. This page names none of that company's products and supports no vendor.
Who this is for: Narayan Iyengar, Freespan's founder. Sections 1 and 2 show how outsiders see the company. Sections 3 and 4 cover the team, how to measure it and who to hire. Section 5 covers the handover to the CTO. The page uses public pages only. It is not a formal investor review.

1 · How Freespan looks from outside

what a first-time visitor learns

The home page tells a clear story. Freespan builds AI for retail only. It sells results, not software licences. It picks one problem with the retailer that can be measured in money. It sends its own people to work inside the retailer. It stays until the retailer's finance team confirms the savings.

Freespan says it brings three things. The first is a retail semantic layer. This is a shared model of what retail terms mean and how they connect, so every AI agent reads a product code or a store group the same way. The second is a library of AI agents for retail tasks. The third is forward deployed engineers (FDEs). These are engineers who work on site with the client and build the system around the client's real process. Freespan runs its software inside the retailer's own cloud account.

Visitors also notice what is missing. The team page names one person. Every open job is a founding role. The site shows no customers, no investors and no product screens. This is normal for a young company. But it means the founder's retail career carries the whole story for now. The diagram below shows the parts the site describes. Read it from top to bottom.

Two points stand out. First, the semantic layer is the only part that makes each new client cheaper. It is also the part the site explains least. Second, the hardest engineering sits on the amber line. Freespan's job posts put SAP tables, EDI feeds (standard files that retailers and suppliers exchange) and flat files in one team. This keeps each client's odd cases out of the shared platform. Section 3 explains how to keep this work easy to reuse.

2 · Claims and evidence

the claims people will test first

Freespan's pitch starts with one point. Most retail work uses several computer systems at once. So a tool that works inside one system cannot automate that work. The site names three groups that fall short. AI assistants from software vendors only see their own vendor's data. Custom AI shops give each retailer a different data model, which is hard to control later. Consultants have no ready-made retail platform.

The founder's essays on the perspectives page make the same case. The August 2026 essay says companies focused on one industry will win, even though they use the same AI models as everyone else. A July essay says most AI pilots fail because of how companies set them up, not because of the models.

The site lists three first projects. Each has a clear measure.

Sourcing the statistics would help. The home page cites a McKinsey survey of 114 buyers. It says they spend 40% of their time combining data, and 71% see little effect from AI buying tools. The page also cites MIT's Project NANDA: AI projects led by vendors reach real use 67% of the time, against 33% for internal builds. This page could not find the original studies. Linking them on the site would remove an easy question.

Showing one piece of the semantic layer would help. One worked example could show how a deduction code, a supplier agreement and a cost file connect. That could turn the least visible claim into the most convincing one.

For the next two years, the team is the product. Until Freespan can name clients, buyers are really buying the people on the team page. The first people they meet will be the team hired until the CTO arrives. So the first hires in section 3 are a sales decision as much as an engineering one.

3 · Kick-start team

build what lasts, and leave the CTO free to choose

Freespan's job openings list eleven role types. They are: architect, full-stack engineers, AI agent engineers, integration engineers, test engineers, infrastructure engineers, a security engineer, a technical product manager, two kinds of FDE, and solutions architects. That is too many specialists for a company with no clients yet.

Until the CTO or technical co-founder joins, a small kick-start team gets the business running: building the first projects and working with clients.

3.1 Until the CTO or technical co-founder joins

Five roles should be enough for the first client, with a sixth if the merchandising project starts early. Most of their work is knowledge about retail and about the client, and that knowledge keeps its value whatever technology comes later.

RoleNumberResponsible for
Retail data modeler1The semantic layer: product groups, supplier cost files, deduction codes, written as definitions and tests
FDE, finance1The first deduction recovery project
FDE, merchandising1The first merchandising report, and learning how the client's buyers work
Senior AI engineer (generalist)1The agents, what each agent sees at each step, and the tests of their answers, built with simple, common tools
Second AI engineerOptionalOnly if the merchandising project starts early, since it relies on AI more than deduction recovery does
Security leadAs neededClient security reviews and early SOC 2 work (a security certification)

The design of what each agent sees at each step, and how its answers are tested, is often called context engineering. It works best with one owner, the senior AI engineer. The FDEs feed it with real cases and client details, rather than each building their own version.

Some of this work will last whatever technology comes later, and some is likely to change. The table below shows which is which.

Likely to lastLikely to change
Written definitions of retail terms and how they connect, with sample data and correct answersThe code that runs the semantic layer
Field-by-field maps from each client's SAP, Oracle Retail and EDI dataThe code that moves the data
Test cases from real client work, including known deduction errors and the answers agents should giveThe agent framework and how agents are run
Maps of how the client's buyers and accounts payable teams really workInfrastructure and install tools
Measured results, client access and client relationshipsTool and vendor choices

The left column is probably where this team's time is best spent. Four rules may help.

Writing the semantic layer as definitions and tests first. A plain document that says what a deduction code is, how it links to an agreement, and what the right answer is for twenty real cases works in any stack. Code built on it can change.

Keeping client-specific logic in tables, not in code. Freespan's job posts already say this about the integration team: "Everything client-specific lives here so it lives nowhere else." Mapping tables can move to a new system. Custom logic spread through code cannot.

Using common tools that are easy to replace. Python, SQL, Postgres and dbt are tools most engineers know, and they are easy to move away from. A custom platform or unusual frameworks can probably wait.

Running the first projects in one environment. Tools to install Freespan in many client clouds can probably wait, since that work is the most likely to change. One setup that the client approves may be enough.

AI coding tools make this small team go further. Tools like Claude Code or Cursor write most of the code. Linear tracks the work: AI agents take tasks, and people review the results. In the author's experience, one strong engineer working this way does the work of two or three engineers in 2023. But AI tools help most with code. They do little for work at the client. That work includes weeks of security review before anyone can read SAP data, sitting with the accounts payable team to map ten years of deduction codes, and earning a buyer's trust to act on an agent's price-cut advice. That is why most of this team faces the client.

3.2 How to know it's working

Two numbers could show whether this plan works. They are worth tracking from the first client.

FDE hours for each new client. This tests the claim that the second client costs much less than the first. Recording it from the first client gives Freespan the proof that investors will ask for in section 2. If the third client takes as many hours as the first, the semantic layer is not being reused.

One semantic layer, not one for each client. Small teams with AI tools write code fast. The risk is no longer speed but too many versions. One owner for the semantic layer would help, along with a firm rule that client-specific logic lives only below the amber line in the first diagram. Without this, each FDE builds a slightly different model for each client. That is the same problem Freespan says custom AI shops create.

4 · Who to hire

a young team, with deep experience in two roles

It may help to hire the roles with the longest-lasting work first: the retail data modeler, then the two FDEs, then the senior AI engineer, with the CTO search running alongside.

Most roles suit people with two to five years of experience who work fast and want ownership. The data modeler and the CTO are different. Every client depends on their decisions, and a wrong data model choice in month three costs a year. These two roles likely need deep experience. The CTO can still have less than 15 years of experience.

Avoid senior titles like head of engineering for the kick-start hires.

The right CTO has 10 to 14 years of experience. This person has led an engineering team. They have shipped software that runs inside customers' systems. They still write code. Look for a head of engineering or principal engineer at a data or retail software company that has raised Series B to D funding. This person is hard to attract. A co-founder role with real equity will likely work better than a senior job.

Real work tends to be the best test. Each trial uses messy, realistic data and allows AI tools. One conversation with a retail operator can add a lot, and so can being clear in job posts about time spent at client sites.

First 90 daysRun the deduction recovery project at the first client, tested against the client's current outside auditor. Show the dollars recovered.
Must have2 to 5 years in recovery audit, accounts payable or deduction work. Writes SQL and Python. Understands shared ad costs, penalty charges and cost-file errors.
Good to haveHas automated part of an audit.
Warning signKnows the accounting but has never automated any of it.

LinkedIn search(PRGX OR "recovery audit" OR "deduction management") AND (SQL OR Python) AND (deduction OR chargeback)

This group is harder to spot and probably more valuable. PRGX hires junior auditors to review retail and grocery payments and supplier deductions, mostly in Excel and Access. Some of them, two or three years in, have learned SQL and Python and already know the area of Freespan's first project. For introductions, start with PRGX's senior technology leaders, such as Amit Dingare, its Chief Technology and Innovation Officer since April 2024. This works better than contacting his team directly.

Good to haveHas presented to a head of merchandising or a similar senior buyer.
Warning signCan't explain how a retail buyer spends their week.
Paid trialShare a week of messy sales and promotion data. Ask for a short margin report, then a 10-minute talk to a retail operator acting as a head of merchandising.
Where to lookCurrent and former FDEs at RELEX and Bluecore.
LinkedIn search("forward deployed" OR "solutions engineer") AND (RELEX OR Bluecore OR "Blue Yonder" OR o9) AND Python

Retail software companies already train people for this role. RELEX's US merchandising FDE role covers production code, data pipelines and planning models for retailers, from the first meeting until the system goes live. Bluecore's FDE job post asks for coding skills at a bootcamp level, and new hires work on client connections and data feeds. So former Bluecore FDEs are a good source of eager engineers who know retail.

First 90 daysBuild the first agents for the first client with simple, common tools. Decide what each agent sees at each step: which definitions, records and past cases, and in what order. Turn real client cases into tests of agent answers. Keep client-specific logic in mapping tables. Document the work so a new CTO can review it in a week.
Must have5 to 10 years of experience. Has built AI agents in production that search for the right information and are checked with tests. Can explain how they decide what an agent sees at each step. Has built data pipelines as well as applications. Uses AI coding tools every day. Is comfortable with a future CTO reviewing, keeping or replacing their work.
Good to haveHas worked with retail or supplier data. Has deployed software into a customer's cloud.
